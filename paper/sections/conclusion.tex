\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We asked whether the performance of graph neural networks for EEG-based dementia classification comes
from how the signal is represented, how the brain graph is built, or the model. On a public
88-subject benchmark, with the model, data and evaluation protocol held fixed and seven
representations crossed with three graph constructions over 25 paired subject-level splits, the
answer is mostly the representation: wavelet and spectral node features lead, graph construction
matters little, and the GCN does not outperform non-graph classifiers on the same features. These
conclusions held with subjects as the unit of inference, on independent splits, under different
stopping rules and preprocessing choices, and --- at the level of the protocol --- in an external
Parkinson's disease cohort, although which representation works best did not transfer between
diseases. The best
configuration, chosen on one set of splits and re-evaluated on an independent set, reached an honest
three-class macro-F1 of \confFOne{}, clearly above chance ($p=\cpermPThreespatialP$) but far below
published figures --- a gap that window-level splitting alone closes on our own pipeline, inflating
subject-level macro-F1 to \cleakPThreespatialLeakySubj{}. A blood--brain-barrier-associated
EEG marker, the paroxysmal slow-wave event, was elevated in AD and FTD under its original definition
but the excess was confined to patients more slowed than any control and disappeared within the
range of slowing shared with controls; it was not elevated in Parkinson's disease, and relative to
each person's background, transient slowing events were less frequent in disease. We release the code, seeds, frozen folds and every raw run so that each number
in this paper can be regenerated from the data.
