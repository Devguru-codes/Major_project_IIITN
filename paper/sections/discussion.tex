\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Principal findings}
Holding the model, data and evaluation protocol fixed, we found four things. First, the node-feature
representation had the largest effect on three-class AD/FTD/control classification, with subjects as the unit
of inference ($F=\mixSelRepF$ and \mixConfRepF{} on two fold sets of the same subjects) and on split-level
macro-F1 (partial $\eta^2=\anovaRepEta$ and \canovaRepEta{}), with wavelet, spectral and fused features
leading. These tests do not allow for the overlap between training sets, and in simulation they declared a
difference between equally informative representations in \simNullSubj--\simNullUncorr\% of datasets; the
calibrated adjustment was not significant on ds004504 ($p\nbSelRepP$ and $p\nbConfRepP$) but was in the
external cohort ($p\nbExtRepP$). A representation effect is therefore established in Parkinson's disease, not
in this dementia cohort, where 88 subjects cannot distinguish the observed spread from sampling variation.
Second, how the graph is built had a detectable but negligible effect: with subjects as units it was
statistically significant but spans at most \mixSelEdgeRange{} in the probability assigned to the true class,
it changes direction between cohorts and does not survive the adjustment for overlapping training sets,
averaged over representations the three graph constructions were formally equivalent within
$\pm\eqMargin$ macro-F1, and within single representations the comparisons were inconclusive rather than
different. The GCN showed no detectable advantage over a random forest or SVM trained
on the same features and subjects, and end-to-end deep EEG networks (EEGNet, a 1D-CNN) on raw windows
did not reach it either; these comparisons, however, could only have detected differences of about
\eqGvcMde{}--\eqDeepMde{} macro-F1, so moderate advantages in either direction remain possible. These conclusions held under three stopping rules, with \SI{5}{\second} to \SI{20}{\second}
windows and without ICA. Third, splitting windows instead
of subjects inflates subject-level macro-F1 from \cleakPThreespatialCorrectSubj{} to \cleakPThreespatialLeakySubj{} on the identical pipeline,
reproducing the top of the published range, although not every published subject-wise figure
(Section~\ref{sec:disc-bench}). Fourth, the
blood--brain-barrier-associated PSWE marker is more frequent in AD and FTD than in controls, but this
excess is explained by continuous background slowing; defined relative to each person's background,
transient slowing events are, if anything, less frequent in dementia.

\subsection{Representation matters more than topology}
On effect sizes, representation outweighed graph construction in every analysis (partial $\eta^2$
\anovaRepEta{} against \anovaEdgeEta{}; adjusted $F$ \nbSelRepF{} against \nbSelEdgeF{}), and the external cohort
confirms that representation can matter; on ds004504 neither effect is statistically established. Within
that limit, the information a GNN can use on this benchmark lives mostly in the node features. Several observations point the same way. Spatial graphs --- which are identical for every
subject and therefore carry no diagnostic information of their own --- did at least as well as
functional and hybrid graphs, and averaged over representations the three graph types were formally
equivalent; within single representations the design could only have detected differences of about
\eqGraphRepMde{} (none of \eqGraphRepN{} comparisons was shown equivalent or different). Neither removing the
graph nor replacing it with a degree-preserving random graph changed performance significantly; any
benefit of message passing is small, and we cannot
attribute it to a particular connectivity pattern rather than to smoothing over neighbouring
electrodes. And non-graph classifiers on flattened features, trained on the same subjects, were not
significantly different from the GCN for any representation, although the GCN on wavelet features was
numerically best in both training regimes and these comparisons cannot exclude differences of about
\eqGvcMde{}. These results agree
with the independent conclusion of the 2026 scoping review that apparent representational gains on
this dataset do not survive leakage-free evaluation \cite{R1}, and they temper the case for
graph-based modelling made in recent work \cite{R2,R8,F7}: on 19-electrode scalp EEG from 88
subjects and with this compact, untuned GCN, we see no evidence that graph structure adds information
beyond good node features. A graph learned from the data, starting from the spatial one, did no better
either (Section~\ref{sec:res-ablations}). These conclusions are scoped to the architecture we fixed: a
two-layer GCN of about 3k parameters, with fixed hyperparameters, on 19 scalp electrodes, and to the graphs
we built: one functional graph (alpha-band wPLI), its product with the spatial graph, and one learned
adjacency. Whether they hold for tuned or deeper graph models, functional graphs from other bands (theta
mattered most to the best model), attention over time, source-space nodes or much larger cohorts
remains open.

\subsection{Neurophysiological interpretation}
Representations that describe each electrode's own spectrum (P1, P3) outperformed one built only from
inter-electrode phase coupling (P2), and the functional graph did not improve on the spatial one.
Within the limits of scalp EEG, this favours local oscillatory slowing over altered large-scale
coupling as the dominant discriminative signal, consistent with spectral slowing being the most
replicated EEG marker of AD \cite{R3,R15} and with the posterior-alpha-to-diffuse-theta shift visible
in our data (Figure~\ref{fig:topo}). Occlusion of the best model points the same way: it relied
mainly on the 4--8 and 8--16 Hz wavelet bands, recognised controls chiefly through theta, and
depended on no single electrode (Figure~\ref{fig:importance}). It does not contradict network accounts of dementia
\cite{F12,F9}: alpha-band wPLI estimated within \SI{10}{\second} windows is noisy, and electrode-level
graphs are a coarse proxy for cortical networks. The error pattern is clinically recognisable:
controls are identified best, while FTD --- the smallest group, and less slowed than AD --- is
identified worst and is usually mistaken for AD. Separating the two dementias remains the hard part of the problem.

\subsection{What the PSWE results mean for an EEG marker of BBB dysfunction}
With the fixed threshold, our data agree in direction with Milikovsky et al.: PSWE rates were roughly
three times higher in AD than in controls after adjusting for age and sex, and FTD --- not previously
studied --- was also elevated. Two further analyses change the interpretation. The fixed-threshold
rate was almost collinear with relative delta+theta power; half of the AD patients were more slowed
than any control, and among patients whose slowing overlapped with controls no excess was detectable
(AD rate ratio \csADRR{}, 95\% CI \csADLo{}--\csADHi{}),
while relative to the events their slowing predicts, patients had fewer. When events were instead defined as transient drops below each channel's own
background, AD and FTD showed fewer of them than controls, under every variant we examined. Together
these results suggest that, in resting scalp EEG with diffuse slowing, a median power frequency below
\SI{6}{\hertz} mostly flags a slow \emph{background} rather than \emph{paroxysmal} events.

We draw three cautious conclusions. First, a fixed-threshold PSWE rate should not be read as evidence
of BBB dysfunction without controlling for background slowing. Second, background-relative
definitions appear necessary if PSWEs are to capture transient cortical dysfunction, although they bring
their own floor effect in very slow recordings, which we observed in the Hz-drop variants. Third, our
data cannot test the BBB link itself: ds004504 contains no BBB imaging, the original detection
details include parameters we could not verify from the full text, and Milikovsky et al.\ studied
different patients under different recording conditions. The decisive test is a cohort with both EEG
and dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI.

\subsection{How results on this benchmark should be read}\label{sec:disc-bench}
On one pipeline, window-level instead of subject-level splitting moved subject-level macro-F1 from
\cleakPThreespatialCorrectSubj{} to \cleakPThreespatialLeakySubj{}, far more than any representation, graph or
architecture change we tested. Figures in the high 90s on ds004504 should therefore be read alongside
their splitting protocol, as argued for this benchmark \cite{R1}, for translational EEG \cite{L2,L3,L4} and
for machine-learning-based science generally \cite{F8,L1,L5}.

Leakage does not, however, account for every published figure. Table~\ref{tab:published} lists the
subject-wise results we could verify from the full texts. Our three-class result (macro-F1 \confFOne{},
accuracy \confAcc{}) is close to the 54\% that the dataset's authors report under leave-subjects-out
validation \cite{P_stefanou}, and well below the 80--84\% of two other reports \cite{P_chen,R2}. Those differ
from our protocol in ways that could each matter. One combines the EEG model's output with the MMSE
\cite{P_chen}, on which every control in this cohort scores 30. The other scores overlapping \SI{20}{\second}
segments rather than subjects, uses OpenNeuro ds006036 rather than ds004504, and evaluates subject-wise a
configuration chosen among 15 in earlier experiments \cite{R2}; per-segment accuracy is not comparable to our
per-subject macro-F1, although our own per-segment accuracy under subject-level splits (\confWinAcc{}) is lower,
not higher, than our per-subject accuracy. From the published descriptions we cannot tell whether
preprocessing or normalisation fitted across subjects also contributes. Our design compares conditions
under one compact, untuned model; it is not a search for the best classifier, and a tuned or pretrained
model could well score higher under the same protocol. At the level reached here (FTD sensitivity
\bestSensFTD{}, expected calibration error \bestEce{}), EEG classifiers of this kind are research benchmarks,
not diagnostic tools. The permutation test and the confirmation folds show only that the result is real and
not an artefact of choosing the best of 21 configurations.

\subsection{What the external cohort adds}
Applying the identical protocol to Parkinson's disease, a different site, device and recording state,
reproduced the protocol-level findings --- a dominant representation effect, a negligible graph
effect, no advantage of the GCN over non-graph twins, inflation under window-level splitting, and a
fixed-threshold PSWE rate that tracks slowing --- but not the detailed ranking of representations
($\rho=\extRepsRho$). The configuration chosen on dementia generalised reasonably (\extSelFOne{},
\extSelRank{}th of 21), and wavelet and fused features were near the top in both cohorts, but
time-domain and connectivity features, weak for dementia, were competitive for Parkinson's disease.
This argues against treating any representation ranking from one disease as a general recommendation:
the evaluation protocol transfers; the best representation is disease- and possibly
recording-specific.

\subsection{Limitations}
The cohort is small, and cross-validation estimates in samples of this size carry wide error bars
\cite{L6}: test folds contain 17--18 subjects, and under the conservative Nadeau--Bengio
correction no pairwise difference between representations, and no single ablation, reached
significance, and the omnibus representation effect on ds004504 did not survive the same adjustment;
equivalence tests show that, apart from the averaged graph-type comparisons, the design could only
have detected differences of roughly \eqAblMde{}--\eqGvcMde{} macro-F1 between individual configurations. We report both corrected and uncorrected tests and give effect sizes, but small
differences between leading representations should not be over-interpreted. All data come from one
site, device and protocol, and FTD is the smallest class ($n=23$). Diagnoses are clinical; the
dataset reports neither medication (cholinesterase inhibitors and psychotropic drugs alter the EEG), nor
FTD subtype, nor biomarker confirmation, so label noise and treatment effects cannot be assessed. The external cohort addresses
generalisation of the protocol but differs in disease, task (eyes open), mains frequency and recording
length (median about \SI{2.5}{\minute}), was recorded with patients on their usual levodopa
\cite{ds004584src}, so medication and disease effects on the EEG cannot be separated, and is two-class,
so it cannot test
whether the AD/FTD results themselves replicate. The confirmation folds re-sample the same 88 subjects, so they remove the
selection bias of choosing among configurations but are not an external replication. Hyperparameters
were fixed a priori and not tuned. Early stopping on a validation set of about 14 subjects selected
a median of \bestEpochMedian{} epochs (\bestEpochLeThree{}\% of runs stopped at epoch 3 or earlier;
median \bestEpochPOne{} for P1 and \bestEpochPThree{} for P3), so many models are lightly trained; however,
training longer (patience 50, or 100 epochs without early stopping) changed no conclusion and did not
help the highest-capacity configuration (raw windows, about 84k parameters), and refitting on training
plus validation subjects for the selected epoch count changed macro-F1 by only \refitMeanGain{} on
average. Calibration was poor. Results are reproducible exactly from the committed feature caches, but rebuilding
the caches from the raw recordings in a later Kaggle environment changed per-split macro-F1 by
\refitRebuiltAbsDiff{} on average (mean \refitRebuiltFOne{} against \refitStoredFOne{} for the best cell;
\refitRebuiltAgree\% of subject decisions unchanged), a reminder that preprocessing libraries are part of
the pipeline. Graphs are
defined on electrodes rather than cortical sources. The demographic floor (\demoFOne) shows residual
age and sex information, which residualisation reduced but did not remove. For PSWEs, the MPF
frequency range was not reported in the source and was set by us (zero-padding the spectrum and
rejecting artefacts did not change the results), we detected events per channel
rather than over averaged regions, the background-relative analysis is post hoc, and no BBB measure
is available.

\subsection{Future work}
The most valuable next study is a multi-site replication of this exact protocol, since a single-site
design cannot establish device or population invariance. Natural extensions are source-space graphs
\cite{F7}, richer learned-graph models than our single trainable adjacency \cite{F4}, spatio-temporal graphs over window sequences \cite{R10,F6},
an architecture sweep under a fixed representation (the transpose of this design) \cite{F5}, an
MCI class, and --- for the BBB question --- simultaneous EEG and dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI to test
whether any PSWE definition tracks measured barrier leakage \cite{B5,B8}.
