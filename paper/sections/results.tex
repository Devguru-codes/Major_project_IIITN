\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Data quality and the confound floor}\label{sec:res-qc}
The fixed preprocessing produced \nWindows{} windows from all 88 subjects (Figure~\ref{fig:cohort});
ICA removed on average \icaExcludedMean{} components per recording. The expected EEG slowing in
dementia was present before any model was trained: the median theta/alpha power ratio was
\thetaAlphaAD{} in AD, \thetaAlphaFTD{} in FTD and \thetaAlphaCN{} in controls, with the posterior alpha
of controls replaced by diffuse delta and theta in AD (Figure~\ref{fig:topo}).

A logistic regression on age and sex alone reached a macro-F1 of \demoFOne{}, against \chanceFOne{}
for a stratified random classifier. Demographics therefore carry some group information, consistent
with the sex imbalance between groups (Table~\ref{tab:demographics}). We use \demoFOne{}, not 1/3, as
the floor that EEG-based models must clear.

\subsection{Representation versus graph construction}\label{sec:res-grid}
Across the 21 cells on the selection folds (\nMainRuns{} runs; Table~\ref{tab:cells}), the best
configuration was \bestCell{}, with subject-level macro-F1 \bestFOne{}~$\pm$~\bestFOneSd{}
(subject-bootstrap 95\% CI \bestFOneCiLo{}--\bestFOneCiHi{}). Because this cell was chosen as the
maximum of 21, that figure is optimistically biased. Re-evaluated on the independent confirmation
folds, the same cell scored \confFOne{}~$\pm$~\confFOneSd{} (95\% CI \confFOneCiLo{}--\confFOneCiHi{};
balanced accuracy \confBalAcc{}, macro ROC-AUC \confAuc{}, Cohen's $\kappa$ \confKappa{}) and again
ranked first of 21. The ordering of all cells was largely preserved (Spearman $\rho=\confRankRho$
between the two fold sets; Figure~\ref{fig:confirm}). We therefore take \confFOne{} as the
selection-free estimate for the best configuration.

Table~\ref{tab:tests} lists every test of the factorial, the primary one first. In the primary
analysis --- the split-level test adjusted for overlapping training sets --- neither representation
($F_\mathrm{NB}=\nbSelRepF$, $p\nbSelRepP$; confirmation \nbConfRepF{}, $p\nbConfRepP$) nor graph construction
($p\nbSelEdgeP$) nor their interaction ($p\nbSelInterP$) was significant on ds004504; in the external cohort
representation was ($p\nbExtRepP$). The unadjusted split-level ANOVA and the by-subject mixed model, which treat
splits or subjects as independent, found strong representation effects in all three analyses, and the mixed
model also a graph effect that spans only \mixSelEdgeRange{} in the probability assigned to the true class;
their details are descriptive and given in the Supplementary results (Table~\ref{tab:mixed},
Figure~\ref{fig:mixed}). The last row of Table~\ref{tab:tests} gives each test's false-positive rate in the
calibration simulation, examined at the end of this section.

On macro-F1 averaged over representations, every pairwise comparison of graph types was formally equivalent within
$\pm\eqMargin$ on both fold sets (TOST with the Nadeau--Bengio correction, largest Holm-corrected $p\eqGraphPMax$
after correction; all corrected 90\% intervals within $\pm$\eqGraphCiMax{}; Table~\ref{tab:equivalence}).
This is positive evidence that, on average, the choice of graph does not matter at that resolution, not merely an
absence of significance. Within single representations the same comparisons were inconclusive: none of the
\eqGraphRepN{} was shown equivalent or different (median detectable difference \eqGraphRepMde{}).

On macro-F1 with the 25 splits as units and without the adjustment (descriptive), representation had a
large effect on both fold sets (Table~\ref{tab:anova}; selection:
$F(\anovaRepDfOne,\anovaRepDfTwo)=\anovaRepF$, Greenhouse--Geisser $p\anovaRepP$, partial
$\eta^2=\anovaRepEta$; confirmation: $F=\canovaRepF$, $p\canovaRepP$, $\eta^2=\canovaRepEta$).
Averaged over graph types, the wavelet (P3, \repPThree{}; confirmation \crepPThree{}), spectral
(P1, \repPOne{}; \crepPOne{}) and fused (P6, \repPSix{}; \crepPSix{}) representations formed a leading
group; raw windows (P5, \repPFive{}) were intermediate; and connectivity strength (P2, \repPTwo{}),
PSWE features (P7, \repPSeven{}) and time-domain statistics (P4, \repPFour{}) trailed
(Figure~\ref{fig:rep}). Graph construction had a small effect on the selection folds
($F(\anovaEdgeDfOne,\anovaEdgeDfTwo)=\anovaEdgeF$, $p\anovaEdgeP$, partial $\eta^2=\anovaEdgeEta$;
marginal macro-F1 \edgespatial{} spatial, \edgefunctional{} functional, \edgehybrid{} hybrid;
Figure~\ref{fig:edge}) that did not replicate on the confirmation folds ($F=\canovaEdgeF$,
$p\canovaEdgeP$; marginal means \cedgespatial{}, \cedgefunctional{} and \cedgehybrid{}). The
representation~$\times$~graph interaction was not significant on either
(selection $F(\anovaInterDfOne,\anovaInterDfTwo)=\anovaInterF$, $p\anovaInterP$; confirmation
$F=\canovaInterF$, $p\canovaInterP$; Figure~\ref{fig:interaction}). Descriptively, a Friedman test across all cells
was significant ($p\friedmanP$), but the Nemenyi critical difference (\cdValue{} ranks) left the top
cells statistically indistinguishable from one another (Figure~\ref{fig:cd}).

Pairwise comparisons depend on how the dependence between cross-validation splits is handled. With
Holm-corrected Wilcoxon tests (descriptive; like the Friedman test they ignore that dependence), \nPairsWilcoxonSig{} of the \nPairs{} representation pairs differed on
the selection folds and \cPairsWilcoxonSig{} on the confirmation folds; with the more conservative
Nadeau--Bengio corrected $t$-test, \nPairsNbSig{} and \cPairsNbSig{} did. For example, P3 exceeded P4
by \pThreeFourDiff{} macro-F1 on the selection folds (Cohen's $d=\pThreeFourD$; Wilcoxon--Holm
$p\pThreeFourWilP$; Nadeau--Bengio--Holm $p\pThreeFourNbP$). The same caveat applies to the
unadjusted omnibus tests, which also treat the splits as independent; with the adjustment (the primary
analysis) no factor was significant. The calibration simulation shows which of these tests can be trusted. When seven representations were
equally informative in the population, the uncorrected split-level test declared a difference in
\simNullUncorr\% of simulated datasets and the by-subject test in \simNullSubj\%, because a representation
that happens to suit this sample keeps its advantage under every re-split of the same subjects; the
adjusted test did so in \simNullNb\%. The spread of representation means under that null, \simNullRange{}
macro-F1, is about the spread we observed (\obsRepRange{}). The price of the adjustment is power: for
true spreads of \simAltRangeLo--\simAltRangeHi{} it detected a difference in only \simAltNbLo--\simAltNbHi\% of
datasets. Real representations are transforms of the same EEG, so their errors are correlated; with the
simulated representations' noise correlated at 0.5 or 0.8, the null spread shrank to
\simCorrRangeLo--\simCorrRangeHi{}, below the observed \obsRepRange{}, but the unadjusted tests still declared a
difference in \simCorrUncorrLo--\simCorrUncorrHi\% (split level) and \simCorrSubjLo--\simCorrSubjHi\% (by subject)
of null datasets, the adjusted test in \simCorrNb\%, and the adjusted test's power rose to
\simCorrNbPowLo--\simCorrNbPowHi\%. The observed spread is therefore larger than strongly correlated but equally
informative representations would produce, yet still not established. The significant subject-level and
uncorrected tests above, and the replication of the ordering
across fold sets of the same subjects ($\rho=\confRankRho$), therefore describe this cohort and do not by
themselves establish a population difference; on ds004504 the representation effect is not established,
and the calibrated evidence that representation matters comes from the external cohort, where the
adjusted test was significant (Section~\ref{sec:res-external}).

\subsection{What the models get right and wrong}\label{sec:res-errors}
For \bestCell{}, sensitivity was \bestSensCN{} for controls, \bestSensAD{} for AD and only
\bestSensFTD{} for FTD (specificities \bestSpecCN{}, \bestSpecAD{} and \bestSpecFTD{}). Across
representations, controls were recognised best and FTD --- the smallest group --- worst; FTD
recordings were most often misclassified as AD, except with PSWE features, where they were more
often taken for controls (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}). The ROC and precision--recall
curves show the same ordering (Figure~\ref{fig:roc}). The models were poorly calibrated (expected
calibration error \bestEce{}), so their probabilities should not be read as clinical risks.

\paragraph{Severity.} Mild patients (MMSE $\ge$ \sevMildMin{}) were not detectably harder to classify than moderate-to-severe patients (none of \sevNTests{} tests significant; Supplementary results and Table~\ref{tab:severity}).

\paragraph{What the best model relies on.} Occluding one wavelet sub-band on every electrode cost
far more than occluding any single electrode (Figure~\ref{fig:importance}). The theta sub-band
\occTopBand{} mattered most (drop in true-class probability \occTopBandDrop{}, 95\% CI
\occTopBandLo{}--\occTopBandHi{}), followed by \occSecondBand{} (\occSecondBandDrop{}); of the five bands,
\occNBandPositive{} had confidence intervals above zero, and the slowest, \occBottomBand{}, mattered least
(\occBottomBandDrop{}). The classes leaned on different bands: controls on \occBandCN{} (\occBandCNDrop{}),
FTD on \occBandFTD{} (\occBandFTDDrop{}) and AD on \occBandAD{} (\occBandADDrop{}). No single electrode
was critical (largest drop \occTopDrop{}; the four most important were \occTopElectrodes{}, and
\occNElecPositive{} of 19 had intervals above zero), so the information is spread across the scalp. That the frontopolar electrodes rank first
may partly reflect residual ocular activity after ICA, and these importances describe this model rather
than the topography of disease.

\subsection{Graph models do not beat their non-graph twins}\label{sec:res-baselines}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines} compares every representation's best GCN cell with classical classifiers
trained on the same features, test splits and weighting, under both training regimes. With training
subjects only, the best classical model reached \classicalTrBest{}; adding the validation subjects
raised it to \classicalTvBest{}, and refitting the GCN on the same subjects raised \bestCell{} to
\refitBestFOne{} (mean gain over all cells \refitMeanGain{}). The paired difference between each
representation's best GCN cell and its best non-graph twin ranged from \fairTrDeltaMin{} to
\fairTrDeltaMax{} in the train-only regime and from \fairTvDeltaMin{} to \fairTvDeltaMax{} in the
train + val regime, and none was significant under either Holm-corrected test (Table~\ref{tab:fair};
for P3, train only: \fairTrPThreeDelta{}, Wilcoxon--Holm $p\fairTrPThreeWilP$; train + val:
\fairTvPThreeDelta{}, $p\fairTvPThreeWilP$). The GCN on wavelet features was numerically the best
model in both regimes, but its advantage over non-graph classifiers is within the noise of this
cohort. Nor could any of these comparisons be shown equivalent: with the corrected standard error the
smallest difference detectable with 80\% power was about \eqGvcMde{} macro-F1
(Table~\ref{tab:equivalence}), so an advantage of the graph model of that order can neither be shown nor
excluded with 88 subjects. The spectral, wavelet, fused and raw representations cleared the demographic floor of
\demoFOne{}; the connectivity (P2), time-domain (P4) and PSWE (P7) representations scored within
about 0.05 of it.

Standard end-to-end EEG networks did no better. On the same raw windows and splits, EEGNet reached
\deepEegnetFOne{}~$\pm$~\deepEegnetSd{} (ROC-AUC \deepEegnetAuc{}) and the 1D-CNN \deepCnnFOne{}~$\pm$~\deepCnnSd{}
(Table~\ref{tab:baselines}; Figure~\ref{fig:deep-confusion}), slightly above the GCN given the same raw
input (\deepEegnetVsRawDelta{} and \deepCnnVsRawDelta{}; Wilcoxon--Holm $p\deepEegnetVsRawWilP$) and below
the GCN on wavelet features (\deepEegnetVsBestDelta{}, $p\deepEegnetVsBestWilP$; \deepCnnVsBestDelta{},
$p\deepCnnVsBestWilP$). The latter difference was significant for the 1D-CNN under the Wilcoxon test
but under neither comparison with the Nadeau--Bengio correction. Learning features end to end from raw
signal therefore did not substitute for a good hand-crafted representation on this cohort, while
using \deepGpuHours{} GPU-hours against none for every other model. As for the classical twins, these
comparisons are imprecise (detectable difference about \eqDeepMde{}), so they show only that the deep
networks are not dramatically better. Training EEGNet with the dropout recommended for cross-subject use
(0.25 instead of the shared 0.5) changed little: macro-F1 \dropFOne{}~$\pm$~\dropSd{}, \dropVsOldDelta{}
against the original EEGNet (Wilcoxon--Holm $p\dropVsOldWilP$) and still \dropVsBestDelta{} below the GCN on
wavelet features ($p\dropVsBestWilP$; Supplementary methods).

\subsection{Ablations}\label{sec:res-ablations}
Table~\ref{tab:ablations} and Figure~\ref{fig:forest} report each ablation on the confirmation folds
as a paired difference from the same cell's confirmation-grid run. No ablation reached significance
under either Holm-corrected test (\cablNSigWil{} of \cablN{} with Wilcoxon, \cablNSigNb{} with
Nadeau--Bengio), and every point estimate except one was negative or zero: no modification improved
on the reference configuration. Removing the graph altogether (identity adjacency) changed
macro-F1 by \cablPThreespatialAOnenographDelta{} [\cablPThreespatialAOnenographLo{},
\cablPThreespatialAOnenographHi{}] for the spatial cell and by \cablPThreehybridAOnenographDelta{}
for the hybrid cell, and a degree-preserving random graph by \cablPThreespatialATworandomgraphDelta{}
and \cablPThreehybridATworandomgraphDelta{}. Any benefit of message passing is therefore small, and
we cannot attribute it to a specific electrode topology rather than to smoothing over neighbours.
Two changes had uncorrected $t$-intervals that excluded zero for the spatial cell: GAT attention
(\cablPThreespatialASixgatDelta{} [\cablPThreespatialASixgatLo{}, \cablPThreespatialASixgatHi{}]) and a
fully connected graph (\cablPThreespatialAThreefullDelta{} [\cablPThreespatialAThreefullLo{},
\cablPThreespatialAThreefullHi{}]), the latter consistent with over-smoothing; neither interval
accounts for the overlap between cross-validation training sets. Sparsity ($k=3$ or 6), binarised
edges, depth and loss weighting changed macro-F1 by at most \cablSmallMax{}. Regressing age and sex out of
every feature cost \cablPThreespatialAOneTworesidualizedDelta{} [\cablPThreespatialAOneTworesidualizedLo{},
\cablPThreespatialAOneTworesidualizedHi{}] for the spatial cell, leaving performance well above the
demographic floor. Shorter (\SI{5}{\second}) or longer (\SI{20}{\second}) windows changed macro-F1 by
\cablPThreespatialASevenwindowFiveDelta{} and \cablPThreespatialASevenwindowTwoZeroDelta{} for the spatial
cell (\cablPThreehybridASevenwindowFiveDelta{} and \cablPThreehybridASevenwindowTwoZeroDelta{} for the
hybrid cell), so \SI{10}{\second} windows were never improved upon. Removing ICA artefact rejection
cost \cablPThreespatialAEightnoicaDelta{} [\cablPThreespatialAEightnoicaLo{},
\cablPThreespatialAEightnoicaHi{}] and \cablPThreehybridAEightnoicaDelta{}, the largest of the
preprocessing changes but still not significant. None of the ablations could be shown equivalent
either (median detectable difference \eqAblMde{}): they exclude large effects of these design choices,
not small ones. The selection-fold ablations
(Table~\ref{tab:ablations-main}) led to the same conclusions.

\paragraph{A learned graph does no better.} Letting the GCN learn its adjacency, starting from the
spatial graph, changed macro-F1 by between \learnDeltaMin{} and \learnDeltaMax{} across the seven
representations on the selection folds (P3: \learnPThree{}, difference \learnPThreeDelta{}) and by
\learnConfDelta{} for P3 on the confirmation folds (\learnConfPThree{}). The ranking of representations
was unchanged (Spearman $\rho=\learnRho$ with the fixed spatial graph). Of the \eqLearnN{} comparisons,
\eqLearnNEquiv{} were formally equivalent within $\pm\eqMargin$ (P1, P2, P6, P7); the other
four (P3, P4 and P5, and P3 on the confirmation folds) had corrected 90\% intervals that included zero
but reached $\pm$\eqLearnCiMax{}, and were inconclusive rather than different
(Table~\ref{tab:equivalence}). A data-driven graph therefore did not
recover information that the fixed constructions missed.

\paragraph{Stopping rule.} Re-running the whole grid with patience 50 or without early stopping left every conclusion unchanged: \bestCell{} remained the best cell, cell rankings were preserved (Spearman $\rho=\esPFiftyRho$ and \esFixedRho{}) and no representation's score changed significantly (Supplementary results).\label{sec:res-es}

\subsection{Leakage inflates results to the top of the published range}\label{sec:res-leak}
Re-running the two best cells on the confirmation folds with window-level splitting --- identical
data, features, graph, model and hyperparameters --- raised subject-level macro-F1 from
\cleakPThreespatialCorrectSubj{} to \cleakPThreespatialLeakySubj{} (spatial) and from
\cleakPThreehybridCorrectSubj{} to \cleakPThreehybridLeakySubj{} (hybrid). Window-level macro-F1, the
metric most often reported, rose to \cleakPThreespatialLeakyWin{} and \cleakPThreehybridLeakyWin{}
(Figure~\ref{fig:leak}). The selection folds gave almost identical values (\leakPThreespatialCorrectSubj{}
to \leakPThreespatialLeakySubj{} for the spatial cell). The leaky protocol thus reproduces the
95--99\% figures reported with segment-level splits on this benchmark. It does not explain every published
subject-wise result, which range from 54\% to 84\% for the three-class problem (Section~\ref{sec:disc-bench}).

\subsection{The best cell is above chance}\label{sec:res-perm}
On the first confirmation repeat, the observed macro-F1 of \bestCell{} was \cpermPThreespatialObs{},
against a label-permutation null with mean \cpermPThreespatialNullMean{} and maximum
\cpermPThreespatialNullMax{} over \cpermPThreespatialN{} permutations ($p=\cpermPThreespatialP$, the
smallest value attainable; Figure~\ref{fig:perm}). The original test on the selection folds gave the
same result (\permPThreespatialN{} permutations, observed \permPThreespatialObs{}, null maximum
\permPThreespatialNullMax{}, $p=\permPThreespatialP$).

\subsection{PSWE: a BBB-associated marker that tracks slowing}\label{sec:res-pswe}
Fixed-threshold PSWEs were most frequent in AD (median \psweRateAD{} events/min), intermediate in FTD
(\psweRateFTD{}) and least frequent in controls (\psweRateCN{}; Kruskal--Wallis $p\psweKwP$;
Figures~\ref{fig:pswe-example} and~\ref{fig:pswe}). In negative-binomial models adjusted for age and
sex, the PSWE rate ratio relative to controls was \psweAdjADRR{} (95\% CI \psweAdjADLo{}--\psweAdjADHi{},
$p\psweAdjADP$) in AD and \psweAdjFTDRR{} (\psweAdjFTDLo{}--\psweAdjFTDHi{}, $p\psweAdjFTDP$) in FTD.

The PSWE rate was, however, almost collinear with background slowing (Spearman $\rho=\psweSlowRho$
with relative delta+theta power; age- and sex-adjusted partial $\rho=\psweSlowPartial$). Once slowing
was added to the model, the AD rate ratio fell below one, to \psweAdjSlowADRR{}
(\psweAdjSlowADLo{}--\psweAdjSlowADHi{}, $p\psweAdjSlowADP$), and FTD to \psweAdjSlowFTDRR{}
($p\psweAdjSlowFTDP$). This is not an artefact of multicollinearity among the predictors --- the
variance inflation of the slowing covariate given group, age and sex was only \colVif{} --- but it does
rest on extrapolation: \colADOutside\% of AD and \colFTDOutside\% of FTD patients were more slowed than
any control. Restricted to the \colADInSupport{} AD and \colFTDInSupport{} FTD patients whose slowing lay
within the control range, no PSWE excess was detectable even before adjusting for slowing (rate ratios
\colSupADRR{}, 95\% CI \csADLo{}--\csADHi{}, $p\colSupADP$, and \colSupFTDRR{}, \csFTDLo{}--\csFTDHi{},
$p\colSupFTDP$); these intervals are wide and do not exclude a moderate excess. Relative to the events that their
slowing predicts --- the slowing--PSWE relation estimated in controls only --- patients had fewer events
than controls (median observed/expected \colOeAD{} for AD and \colOeFTD{} for FTD versus \colOeCN{};
Kruskal--Wallis $p\colOeP$). The PSWE rate was not
significantly associated with MMSE within patients ($\rho=\psweMmseRho$, $p\psweMmseP$). In the
classifier, PSWE features alone (P7) performed near the demographic floor (\repPSeven{}), and adding
them to the best representation, or using PSWE co-occurrence as the graph, did not help
(Section~\ref{sec:res-ablations}).

\paragraph{Detector robustness.} Neither the coarse MPF grid nor artefacts explain these results. With
zero-padded spectra and artefact rejection together (a median of at most \psweRobRejectPct\% of segments
rejected per group), rates correlated $\rho=\psweRobRhoMain$ with the original ones, the age- and sex-adjusted
rate ratios were \psweRobAdjADRR{} for AD ($p\psweRobAdjADP$) and \psweRobAdjFTDRR{} for FTD
($p\psweRobAdjFTDP$), and adjusting for slowing again brought them below one (\psweRobAdjSlowADRR{} and
\psweRobAdjSlowFTDRR{}). Without ICA, the rate ratios were \noIcaPsweADRR{} and \noIcaPsweFTDRR{}
($\rho=\noIcaPsweSlowRho$ with slowing), so ocular artefacts do not create the excess either. Nor does
the event rule (Table~\ref{tab:pswe-grid}): across the \gridN{} combinations of 5--\SI{7}{\hertz}
thresholds and 3--\SI{8}{\second} minimum durations (A11), the age- and sex-adjusted AD rate ratio ranged
from \gridADRrMin{} to \gridADRrMax{} (significant in \gridADNSig{} of \gridN{}), FTD from \gridFTDRrMin{} to
\gridFTDRrMax{} (\gridFTDNSig{} of \gridN{}), the correlation with slowing from \gridRhoMin{} to \gridRhoMax{},
and every slowing-adjusted AD ratio was below one (\gridADSlowMin{}--\gridADSlowMax{}).

\paragraph{Background-relative PSWEs (exploratory).} Defined relative to each channel's own background, transient slowing events were less frequent in AD and FTD than in controls under every variant (Supplementary results).

\subsection{External validation: Parkinson's disease (ds004584)}\label{sec:res-external}
The ds004584 recordings passed the same quality gates (\extNWindows{} windows from 149 subjects;
\extIcaExcluded{} ICA components removed per recording on average), and theta/alpha slowing was again
higher in patients (\extThetaAlphaPD{}) than in controls (\extThetaAlphaCN{}). Age and sex alone
reached a macro-F1 of \extDemoFOne{} against \extChanceFOne{} for chance in this two-class problem.

\paragraph{Protocol effects replicated.} Here the representation effect survived the primary,
overlap-adjusted test ($F_\mathrm{NB}=\nbExtRepF$, $p\nbExtRepP$), unlike on ds004504. Descriptively, in the
by-subject mixed model, $F(\mixExtRepDf,\mixExtRepDenDf)=\mixExtRepF$ with \mixExtNPairsSig{} of 21 pairs
separated, whereas graph construction was small (marginal means \mixExtMspatial{} spatial,
\mixExtMfunctional{} functional, \mixExtMhybrid{} hybrid; $p\mixExtEdgeP$) and favoured a different graph
type than on ds004504; on macro-F1 with splits as units, representation was significant
($F=\extAnovaRepF$, $p\extAnovaRepP$, partial $\eta^2=\extAnovaRepEta$) and graph construction was not
($p\extAnovaEdgeP$) in the unadjusted analysis. The GCN was not significantly better than non-graph classifiers trained on the
same subjects (differences \extGvcDeltaMin{} to \extGvcDeltaMax{}, none significant after Holm correction).
Window-level splitting of the configuration selected on ds004504 inflated subject-level macro-F1 from
\extSelFOne{} to \extLeakySubj{} (window-level \extLeakyWin{}), in line with an independent audit of the
same cohort \cite{L3}.

\paragraph{The ranking only partly transferred.} The configuration selected on ds004504
(P3~$\times$~spatial), evaluated without re-selection, reached macro-F1 \extSelFOne{}~$\pm$~\extSelFOneSd{}
(subject-bootstrap 95\% CI \extSelCiLo{}--\extSelCiHi{}; balanced accuracy \extSelBalAcc{}, ROC-AUC
\extSelAuc{}), well above its permutation null (observed \extPermObs{} on the first repeat against a
null maximum of \extPermNullMax{}; $p=\extPermP$, \extPermN{} permutations) and ranked \extSelRank{}th of
21; the best cell was \extBestCell{} (\extBestFOne{}). Wavelet and fused features were among the top three on both datasets
(Table~\ref{tab:external}), but the full ordering did not transfer: Spearman $\rho=\extCellsRho$
($p\extCellsRhoP$) across the 21 cells and $\rho=\extRepsRho$ ($p\extRepsRhoP$) across the seven
representations, mainly because time-domain (P4) and connectivity (P2) features, near the bottom on
ds004504, ranked third and fourth on ds004584, and raw windows (P5) fell to last (Figure~\ref{fig:external}).

\paragraph{PSWEs in Parkinson's disease.} Fixed-threshold PSWEs were not more frequent in Parkinson's
disease than in controls (median \extPsweRatePD{} versus \extPsweRateCN{} events/min; age- and
sex-adjusted rate ratio \extPsweAdjRR{}, 95\% CI \extPsweAdjLo{}--\extPsweAdjHi{}, $p\extPsweAdjP$),
consistent with the earlier report that they are uncommon in this disease \cite{B3}. As on ds004504, the
rate tracked background slowing ($\rho=\extPsweSlowRho$), and adjusting for slowing brought the ratio below one
(\extPsweAdjSlowRR{}, $p\extPsweAdjSlowP$). The rate was not associated with MoCA within patients
($\rho=\extPsweMocaRho$, $p\extPsweMocaP$), no background-relative variant differed between groups
(smallest $p\extRelMinP$), and zero-padding with artefact rejection left the result unchanged (rate
ratio \psweExtRobAdjPDRR{}, $p\psweExtRobAdjPDP$). No event rule of the A11 grid produced a
significant Parkinson's excess (rate ratios \gridExtPDRrMin{}--\gridExtPDRrMax{}), almost all patients lay
within the controls' range of slowing (\colExtPDInSupport{} of 100), and relative to their slowing they had
fewer events than controls (median observed/expected \colExtOePD{} versus \colExtOeCN{}, $p\colExtOeP$).
