\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We asked whether the performance of graph neural networks for EEG-based dementia classification comes
from how the signal is represented, how the brain graph is built, or the model. On a public
88-subject benchmark, with a compact, untuned model, the data and the evaluation protocol held fixed and seven
representations crossed with three graph constructions over 25 paired subject-level splits, the
representation had by far the larger effect: wavelet and spectral node features led, graph construction
had a detectable but negligible effect, and the GCN did not outperform non-graph classifiers on the same features. Once the
overlap between training sets is accounted for --- uncorrected tests, a simulation showed, find
differences between equally informative representations in most datasets --- the representation effect
is established in an external Parkinson's disease cohort but not on ds004504, and which representation
works best did not transfer between diseases.
The best configuration, chosen on one set of splits and re-evaluated on an independent set, reached a
three-class macro-F1 of \confFOne{}, clearly above chance ($p=\cpermPThreespatialP$). Window-level splitting
inflated it to \cleakPThreespatialLeakySubj{} on the same pipeline, reproducing the upper end of published
figures; published subject-wise results range more widely than leakage alone explains. A
blood--brain-barrier-associated EEG marker, the paroxysmal slow-wave event, was elevated in AD and FTD under
its original definition, but the excess tracked background slowing and was not detectable among patients
whose slowing overlapped with controls'; it was not elevated in Parkinson's disease, and relative to each
person's background, transient slowing events were less frequent in disease. We release the code, seeds,
frozen folds and every raw run so that each number in this paper can be regenerated from the data.
